
KEY DISCOVERIES FROM THE FOUR PLOTS:

📊 PLOT 1 (Support vs Confidence):
Discovery: Most rules have high support (>50%) but confidence near baseline (14%)
Implication: Common patterns exist, but they don't predict diabetes better than random
Insight: This is why Confidence alone is misleading - needs Lift for context

📊 PLOT 2 (Support vs Lift):  
Discovery: 70.7% of rules have Lift≈1 regardless of support level
Implication: Prevalence ≠ Predictive power
Insight: Even patterns affecting 90% of diabetics might be irrelevant (Lift=1)
Example: "Has healthcare" is common but doesn't distinguish diabetics from non-diabetics

📊 PLOT 3 (Confidence vs Lift):
Discovery: Rules with 60-70% confidence can have Lift=1 (no association)
Implication: High accuracy doesn't mean causation or even correlation
Insight: This is THE most important plot for avoiding spurious rules
Why: Baseline diabetes rate is 14%, so even random guessing can get 60% confidence
      if we predict "has other common conditions"

📊 PLOT 4 (Lift Distribution):
Discovery: 70.7% rules are "noise" (Lift 0.95-1.05)
            28.6% show positive association (Lift>1.05)
            0.6% show protective effect (Lift<0.95)
Implication: Apriori algorithm generates MANY false positives
Insight: This justifies using min_lift threshold in real applications
Recommendation: Only analyze rules with Lift<0.9 or Lift>1.1

🎯 OVERALL CONCLUSION:
Why we need ALL THREE metrics:
  - Support alone: Would accept "Everyone has healthcare" as a good rule
  - Confidence alone: Would accept rules that just predict common outcomes
  - Lift alone: Could miss important high-impact but rare rules
  
✓ Optimal approach: Filter by minimum Lift first, THEN rank by Support×Confidence

WHY NOT USE THRESHOLDS IN VISUALIZATION?
Answer: Thresholds are arbitrary and hide data structure
Better: Show ALL rules, let patterns emerge naturally
The clustering around Lift=1 is itself a finding (most rules are noise)
