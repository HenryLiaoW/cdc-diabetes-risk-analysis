
CRITICAL QUESTIONS FOR REFLECTION (Include in your report):

1. CORRELATION IS NOT CAUSATION
   Q: If we find "Healthy Diet (Fruits+Veggies) → Diabetes" with Lift<1, 
      does it mean eating vegetables PREVENTS diabetes?
   A: Not necessarily! Possible explanations:
      - People diagnosed with diabetes START eating healthier (reverse causation)
      - Wealthy people afford both healthy food AND better healthcare (confounding)
      - Survivorship bias: People who died from diabetes are not in the survey
   
2. TEMPORAL AMBIGUITY
   Q: BRFSS is a cross-sectional survey (one point in time). Can we determine 
      which came first: the risk factor or the disease?
   A: No. We cannot distinguish between:
      - Risk factors that CAUSE diabetes
      - Behavioral changes AFTER diabetes diagnosis
   
3. SIMPSON'S PARADOX
   Q: A rule might show positive association overall, but negative within subgroups.
      Example: "Exercise → Diabetes" might seem positive overall, but negative 
      within each age group separately.
   A: Always stratify by confounders (age, sex, income) before drawing conclusions.
   
4. SELECTION BIAS (Telephone Survey)
   Q: Who is more likely to answer a phone survey?
   A: Older adults, homeowners, less tech-savvy individuals. This means:
      - Young diabetics might be underrepresented
      - True diabetes prevalence in 18-30 age group might be higher than observed
   
5. SELF-REPORTED DATA RELIABILITY
   Q: Can people accurately report their BMI, diet, and exercise?
   A: Studies show self-reported BMI is often underestimated by 2-3 kg/m².
      This means our "BMI>30" group might actually include BMI>32-33.

RECOMMENDATION FOR PUBLIC HEALTH POLICY:
- Use "Golden Rules" (high support, moderate lift) for population screening
- Use "Extreme High-risk" rules for targeted intensive interventions
- Conduct longitudinal studies to confirm causality before implementing prevention programs
- Consider socioeconomic confounders when designing interventions
